Session 026 --- Canonical Reasoning Graph

Date: 2026-08-15
Status: PART I FROZEN / PASS
Phase: Canonical Reasoning Graph & Build Architecture

Purpose

Session 026 begins the transition from defining what Management
Intelligence™ V5 understands to defining how MI reasons from business
reality to management intelligence, management action, observed
outcomes, and learning.

The governing purpose is:

MI's purpose is not to find something wrong. MI's purpose is to
determine what management needs to understand in order to make a
better decision.

This session establishes Part I of the Canonical Reasoning Graph:
Diagnostic Reasoning Architecture.

1. Management Decision Intelligence Principle

The purpose of Management Intelligence is not to produce more
information, identify more problems, or generate more recommendations.
Its purpose is to improve the quality of management decisions.

MI is therefore not an alert engine, dashboard replacement, or automated
critic.

Its responsibility is to identify the smallest number of material,
evidence-supported management conditions that deserve attention and
explain them at the appropriate management altitude.

2. Outcome Does Not Establish Cause

An unfavorable result is an Outcome.

It is not automatically the cause of the result.

Example:

A dealership misses tire sales.

Possible upstream explanations include:

insufficient eligible vehicle population;

incomplete evaluation;

opportunity not identified;

estimate not created;

recommendation not presented;

customer decline;

authorized work not completed;

parts availability;

data integrity;

vehicle or customer mix.

MI must reason upstream before assigning causal meaning.

Outcome ≠ Cause Principle

An unfavorable Outcome does not establish the Condition causing it.
MI must reason upstream from the Outcome before assigning causal
meaning.

3. Integrity Gate

Before MI interprets business evidence, it must determine whether the
evidence is sufficiently reliable for the strength of conclusion being
considered.

The Integrity Gate contains five primary tests.

3.1 Population Integrity

Is MI measuring the correct population?

Examples:

RO Count is not VIN Count.

VIN Count is not Unique VIN Count.

CP population is not automatically total Vehicles Serviced.

Opportunity-eligible population is not automatically all ROs.

3.2 Evidence Integrity

Is the evidence sufficiently reliable and internally coherent?

MI considers:

source authority;

data mapping;

completeness;

conflicting records;

known data-quality issues;

unusual coding;

reconciliation.

3.3 Temporal Integrity

Does the evidence represent the appropriate period and effective
business state?

MI must not casually combine evidence from incompatible periods or apply
a current organizational structure to historical evidence without
effective-dated lineage.

3.4 Comparison Integrity

If MI says something is high, low, better, worse, unusual, or different,
is the comparison legitimate?

Comparison context may include:

brand;

vehicle population;

work type;

tenure;

role;

geography;

seasonality;

operating structure;

relevant peer population.

3.5 Evidence Sufficiency

Is there enough evidence to support the strength of conclusion MI
intends to make?

Weak evidence may support:

Possible discovery constraint.

It may not support:

Tire discovery is the primary causal constraint.

Integrity Rule

Diagnostic strength may not exceed the integrity and sufficiency of
the evidence supporting it.

4. Detection

Detection is the lowest diagnostic reasoning threshold.

Definition

Detection is the evidence-supported identification that a material
Condition, difference, change, opportunity, strength, risk,
constraint, or anomaly exists.

Example:

Technician tire-opportunity discovery is materially below a
sufficiently comparable technician population.

Detection establishes what is different.

It does not establish why.

5. Materiality Gate

Not every detectable difference deserves management attention.

MI evaluates whether the Condition is sufficiently meaningful to the
business.

Materiality may include:

economic materiality;

operating materiality;

customer materiality;

safety relevance;

capacity impact;

people/leadership impact;

strategic materiality;

persistence;

acceleration;

organizational reach;

emerging consequence;

decision relevance.

Management Relevance Principle

MI does not convert every detectable difference into management
intelligence. A Condition must be sufficiently material, actionable,
consequential, persistent, emerging, or decision-relevant to justify
management attention.

The desired behavior is deliberate silence when a difference is
statistically detectable but operationally irrelevant.

6. Condition Establishment

After passing the Integrity and Materiality Gates, MI establishes the
evidence-supported Condition without prematurely assigning cause.

Example:

Observed Evidence: > Technician A discovers tire opportunities on 4% of
eligible inspections.

Derived Evidence: > Sufficiently comparable technicians average 12%.

Supported Condition: > A material technician-level tire-discovery
difference exists.

Unsupported premature conclusion: > Technician A has an
inspection-execution problem.

The latter requires causal reasoning.

7. Detection → Explanation → Causation

MI separates three reasoning stages.

Detection

What is happening or different?

Explanation

Which part of the operating pathway best explains the observed result?

Example:

Tire performance is best explained by lower opportunity discovery
rather than downstream presentation or conversion.

Causation

What sufficiently supported upstream Condition is reasonably expected to
influence the downstream result?

Example:

Inspection-level evidence shows:

tire measurements recorded on 43% of eligible inspections;

comparable technicians record measurements on 94%;

when measurements are actually completed, the red/yellow
distribution is similar to peers.

MI may then infer:

Lower tire discovery is primarily associated with incomplete tire
evaluation rather than materially different vehicle need.

With sufficient evidence, MI may identify:

Primary controllable causal condition: incomplete tire evaluation.

8. Competing Explanation Test

Before MI assigns strong causal meaning, it evaluates reasonable
alternative explanations supported by the business context.

Examples may include:

vehicle mix;

mileage mix;

customer mix;

work type;

assignment pattern;

inspection-tool failure;

data mapping;

technician behavior;

advisor behavior;

parts availability;

facility/capacity constraints;

external events.

A diagnosis becomes stronger as credible competing explanations are
evaluated and weakened.

Competing Explanation Principle

Before assigning strong causal meaning, MI evaluates material
alternative explanations supported by the available business context
and reduces diagnostic confidence when meaningful alternatives remain
unresolved.

9. Causal Strength

MI does not treat every relationship as causal.

Four canonical levels are established.

9.1 Association

A and B move together.

No causal claim is made.

9.2 Contributing Condition

Evidence supports B as one meaningful contributor to A.

Other contributors may exist.

9.3 Probable Primary Condition

Available evidence indicates B is currently the strongest supported
explanation for A.

This is expected to be a common management-level conclusion.

9.4 Defensible Root Cause

Evidence supports B as an upstream causal Condition whose correction is
reasonably expected to materially alter the downstream Outcome, with
meaningful competing explanations sufficiently evaluated.

This is intentionally a high evidentiary bar.

10. Diagnostic Unknown

MI is permitted to conclude that the available evidence does not support
a sufficiently reliable causal explanation.

Definition

Diagnostic Unknown is a detected Condition for which available
evidence is insufficient to identify a sufficiently supported causal
explanation.

MI may then identify the additional evidence needed.

Example:

Cause not yet sufficiently established. Inspection-level tire
measurements by technician are required to distinguish evaluation
behavior from vehicle-population differences.

Diagnostic Restraint Principle

When available evidence cannot support a sufficiently reliable
causal conclusion, MI identifies the uncertainty rather than
manufacturing a diagnosis.

Unknown is a valid intelligence state.

11. Controllability / Influenceability

Cause and management influence are not the same thing.

Example:

Causal Condition: > Regional snowstorm materially reduced customer
arrivals.

Management cannot correct the weather.

MI therefore identifies the portion of the causal pathway management can
influence.

Potential controllable Condition: > Appointment and customer-recovery
process after the disruption.

Controllability Principle

MI distinguishes what caused an Outcome from the portion of the
causal pathway management can reasonably influence.

This protects management from being judged for uncontrollable events
while still identifying appropriate management response.

12. Responsible Management Role

After determining controllability, MI identifies the lowest appropriate
management role with sufficient responsibility, authority, or influence
to address the Condition.

Examples:

technician inspection execution → Service Manager;

parts stocking discipline → Parts Manager;

shared system configuration affecting many stores → appropriate
system/enterprise owner;

strategic capital decision → GM, owner, executive, or other
authorized role depending on tenant structure.

Lowest Responsible Altitude Principle

MI directs intelligence to the lowest management role with
sufficient authority and capability to address the material
Condition.

This principle remains scale-neutral.

A one-rooftop tenant does not require artificial organizational
hierarchy.

13. Root-Cause Compression

Multiple downstream symptoms may share one upstream Condition.

Example:

A dealership shows:

low tire performance;

low brake performance;

low battery performance;

low video creation;

low MPI completion.

A conventional dashboard produces five red indicators.

MI discovers that a small number of technicians account for most
incomplete inspection execution.

The upstream Condition may explain all five downstream Outcomes.

Root-Cause Compression Principle

When multiple downstream symptoms share a sufficiently supported
upstream Condition, MI prioritizes the smallest number of upstream
management pathways capable of materially improving the broader
system.

MI should therefore avoid five separate recommendations when one
upstream management pathway is better supported.

14. Management Pathway

After causal reasoning, controllability, responsible-role assignment,
and Root-Cause Compression, MI identifies the smallest defensible
management pathway reasonably expected to improve the Condition.

A Management Pathway is not automatically a management Decision.

MI may recommend.

Authorized management decides.

The pathway should be:

evidence-supported;

appropriately scoped;

role-relevant;

materially connected to the Condition;

proportional to confidence;

explicit about assumptions and unknowns;

designed to influence the upstream Condition rather than merely
cosmetically improve the downstream metric.

15. Intelligence Object

Only after completing the applicable reasoning gates should MI
communicate a material diagnostic conclusion as an Intelligence Object.

The Intelligence Object retains:

supporting Evidence;

applicable Entity/Relationships;

detected Condition;

materiality;

competing explanations considered;

causal-strength classification;

confidence;

controllability/influenceability;

Responsible Management Role;

Root-Cause Compression relationships;

recommended Management Pathway where appropriate;

unknowns;

required additional evidence;

reasoning lineage;

expiration/revalidation state.

19. Recommendation Construction

MI does not automatically recommend an action every time it creates
Intelligence.

Legitimate outputs include:

continue current practice;

no management intervention is presently justified;

additional evidence is required;

monitor;

select among reasonable alternatives;

take a specific management action.

When intervention is justified, MI creates a Recommended Management
Pathway, not a command.

A material recommendation should identify:

Condition being addressed;

supporting Evidence;

expected effect;

Responsible Management Role;

urgency;

confidence;

material Assumptions;

dependencies;

known trade-offs;

reasonable alternatives;

success/evaluation method.

Recommendation Proportionality Principle

The specificity and strength of an MI recommendation may not exceed
the strength of the evidence, causal confidence, controllability, and
expected material benefit supporting it.

20. Management Alternatives

For material Decisions, MI may retain reasonable alternatives rather
than presenting false certainty.

Examples for a capacity constraint may include:

recruit technicians;

extend productive hours;

improve current utilization;

add bays/equipment;

No-Action.

Alternatives may be compared using:

Evidence;

Assumptions;

expected effect;

timing;

cost;

risk;

strategic consequence;

reversibility;

optionality;

confidence.

MI may recommend. Authorized management selects.

21. Management Decision

Management may:

Accept;

Modify;

Select an alternative;

Reject;

Defer;

Request more evidence;

Choose No-Action.

The Decision lineage preserves:

What MI recommended + what management knew + what management
decided + why + what was expected.

MI recommendation and Management Decision are never silently treated as
the same object or state.

This Decision lineage supports the Management Decision Journal and
long-term institutional management memory.

22. Management Challenge

Management may possess context MI does not yet have.

Example:

MI recommends coaching a technician for low inspection completion.

The Service Manager reports that the technician's inspection tablet has
malfunctioned for three days.

That Management Judgment becomes governed Declared Evidence and may
reopen MI reasoning.

Canonical loop:

Intelligence v1 → Management Judgment → New Evidence → Re-evaluation
→ Intelligence v2

Management Challenge Principle

Management may challenge MI intelligence by providing additional
evidence, context, judgment, or alternative reasoning. MI evaluates
that contribution as governed evidence rather than treating
disagreement as noncompliance.

MI and management are not adversaries. Management can teach MI.

23. Intervention Lifecycle

Once management selects an action, the Intervention lifecycle may
include:

Recommended → Accepted → Scheduled → Executed → Completed →
Evaluated

These states remain distinct.

A recommendation does not prove execution.

Execution does not prove execution quality.

Completion does not prove effectiveness.

24. Execution Verification Gate

Before MI evaluates whether an Intervention worked, it determines
whether the Intervention was actually executed sufficiently to test the
recommendation.

Canonical execution states include:

Verified Execution

Evidence supports meaningful execution.

Partial Execution

Evidence supports some execution but not enough to test the intended
effect reliably.

Unverified Execution

MI lacks sufficient evidence to determine whether execution occurred.

Non-Execution

Evidence supports that the selected Intervention was not executed.

Execution Before Effectiveness Principle

MI does not attribute an observed Outcome to an Intervention unless
available evidence sufficiently establishes that the Intervention
occurred with enough fidelity and duration to reasonably test its
expected effect.

This prevents MI from incorrectly learning that a recommendation failed
when it was never meaningfully implemented.

25. Expected Effect

Every material Intervention should define its expected effect before MI
observes the eventual result.

Example:

Intervention: > Coach technician on tire evaluation.

Expected Condition Change: > Tire measurement completion materially
improves toward sufficiently comparable technician behavior.

Expected Intermediate Effect: > Legitimate tire-opportunity discovery
increases.

Expected Downstream Outcome: > Tire sales opportunity and appropriate
completed work improve.

MI evaluates the causal chain rather than jumping directly from action
to final KPI.

26. Condition Effect Before Outcome Effect

MI first evaluates whether the Intervention changed the Condition it was
intended to change.

Example:

tire measurement completion: 43% → 91%;

legitimate opportunity discovery increases;

tire sales remain flat.

The Intervention may have successfully changed the targeted Condition
even though the final business Outcome did not yet improve.

This reveals a possible downstream constraint rather than proving the
Intervention failed.

Intervention Effectiveness

Did the Intervention materially change the Condition it was intended
to change?

Outcome Effectiveness

Did the resulting Condition change produce the expected downstream
business Outcome?

These are separate evaluations.

27. Constraint Migration

When an upstream Condition improves but the desired Outcome does not, MI
reopens causal reasoning at the next unresolved stage.

Example:

Inspection completion fixed → Discovery improves → Presentation
remains weak.

MI may conclude:

Primary constraint migrated downstream from technician evaluation to
advisor presentation.

Constraint Migration Principle

When an upstream Condition materially improves but the desired
Outcome does not, MI re-evaluates the causal pathway to determine
whether the primary constraint has migrated to another stage.

MI follows the constraint rather than permanently labeling the store or
person with the original symptom.

28. No-Action Evaluation

No-Action is a legitimate Decision and remains subject to outcome
evaluation.

If management chooses No-Action because the Condition is stable,
immaterial, uncontrollable, or intervention value is insufficient, MI
records the rationale and expected state.

Future Outcomes test the Decision without retrospective distortion.

Decision Quality ≠ Outcome Quality

A sound Decision can produce an unfavorable Outcome because of
uncertainty or subsequent Events.

A poor Decision can produce a favorable Outcome through luck.

MI evaluates Decision Quality using the Evidence, Assumptions,
alternatives, trade-offs, and uncertainty available at the time of the
Decision, not merely the eventual Outcome.

29. Four Effectiveness Dimensions

MI evaluates effectiveness across four separate dimensions.

A. Execution Effectiveness

Was the selected Intervention actually performed adequately?

B. Condition Effectiveness

Did the targeted Condition change as expected?

C. Outcome Effectiveness

Did the expected downstream business Outcome occur?

D. Intelligence Effectiveness

Was MI's diagnosis and/or recommendation useful in improving the
management Decision?

These dimensions must not be collapsed into one simplistic score at the
reasoning layer.

30. MI Effectiveness

MI evaluates its own usefulness using the full Decision and Intervention
lineage.

Examples:

MI correct + management executes + Outcome improves

Strong positive MI-effectiveness evidence.

MI correct + management does not execute

The Outcome does not provide a valid test of recommendation
effectiveness.

MI diagnosis wrong + management executes faithfully + target Condition does not change

Negative MI-effectiveness evidence.

MI recommendation rejected + management provides better context + alternate management action succeeds

MI learns from management.

MI Effectiveness therefore measures whether MI is improving management
decisions, not whether managers obediently follow MI recommendations.

31. Learning From Management

Management is a source of governed learning.

When experienced managers repeatedly make successful Decisions under
sufficiently similar Conditions, MI may detect that Management Judgment
outperforms its current recommendation logic.

Canonical learning path:

Management Decision → Context → Intervention → Outcome → Replication
→ Pattern Candidate

With sufficient validation, MI may update future reasoning.

The machine is not assumed to be the permanent teacher.

32. Pattern Maturity

Patterns mature through governed stages.

Observation

A relationship occurred once. No generalization.

Pattern Candidate

The relationship recurs sufficiently to justify further evaluation.

Validated Pattern

The relationship persists with adequate Evidence and sufficiently
understood context.

Operational Wisdom

A Validated Pattern demonstrates sufficient:

persistence;

replication;

transferability;

causal plausibility;

outcome effectiveness;

context boundaries;

known exceptions.

Operational Wisdom is earned through evidence. It is not manually
declared into existence.

33. Transferability Gate

Before applying a successful Intervention or Pattern from one Entity to
another, MI evaluates whether the material governing Conditions are
sufficiently similar.

Relevant context may include:

brand;

volume;

staffing;

capability;

facility;

customer mix;

geography;

leadership;

technology;

process;

economic structure;

organizational context.

Transferability Principle

MI does not generalize a successful Intervention beyond its
validated context unless evidence supports sufficient similarity in
the material Conditions governing its effectiveness.

This prevents "it worked there" from becoming causal science.

34. Pattern Decay and Retirement

Operational Wisdom remains subject to continuing Evidence.

Patterns retain:

last validation;

recency;

continued performance;

context drift;

confidence;

exceptions;

applicability boundaries.

A Pattern may transition:

Validated → Weakened → Context-Limited → Superseded / Retired

Wisdom Decay Principle

Operational Wisdom remains subject to continuing evidence and may be
weakened, narrowed, superseded, or retired when its predictive or
management value deteriorates.

35. MI Self-Correction

When later Evidence shows prior Intelligence was incomplete or
incorrect, MI does not overwrite history.

Example:

Intelligence v1: > Advisor presentation is the primary constraint.

Intervention executes.

Presentation improves.

Outcome does not.

New Evidence establishes technician coding as the more important causal
Condition.

MI creates Intelligence v2 and supersedes v1 while preserving:

Evidence originally available;

missing Evidence;

reasoning path;

original confidence;

recommendation;

management Decision;

execution;

observed effects;

new Evidence;

corrected diagnosis.

MI may classify the reasoning failure, for example:

insufficient evidence;

bad source;

missing domain evidence;

faulty comparison;

weak causal rule;

unseen Event;

invalid Assumption;

context shift.

Reasoning Error Classification is a classification of MI Effectiveness
evidence, not another foundational object.

36. Forecast and Scenario Learning

Forecasts and Scenarios use the same learning architecture.

A Forecast miss does not automatically establish reasoning failure.

MI evaluates:

which Assumptions held;

which Assumptions failed;

whether a subsequent Event changed the environment;

whether causal relationships were misestimated;

whether Evidence quality was insufficient;

whether the model itself was wrong.

Example:

Forecast: > +8% demand.

Actual: > +1%.

Subsequent Event: > OEM ends recall campaign early.

MI distinguishes an invalidated Assumption caused by a later Event from
poor original forecasting logic.

Part III --- Cross-Domain Orchestration

37. Domains Contribute Evidence; Management Receives Unified Intelligence

The twelve MI domains are specialized reasoning lenses over one
canonical business reality.

Example:

Domain 7 detects low opportunity discovery;

Domain 4 identifies technician inspection behavior;

Domain 5 finds no material parts constraint;

Domain 6 finds healthy customer communication;

Domain 9 quantifies economic consequence.

MI should not present five independent management problems.

It synthesizes the evidence into the smallest coherent Intelligence
Object.

Example:

Tire performance is primarily constrained by inconsistent technician
evaluation rather than advisor presentation, customer response, or
parts availability. A small group of technicians accounts for most
missed discovery. Restoring inspection execution is the highest-value
current management pathway.

The manager receives Decision Intelligence, not the internal
organizational chart of MI.

38. Cross-Domain Arbitration

Canonical arbitration order:

1. Shared Condition Detection

Are multiple domains describing the same underlying Condition?

2. Causal Altitude

Which Evidence is closest to the earliest material controllable
Condition?

3. Materiality

Which Condition matters most?

4. Root-Cause Compression

Can multiple findings collapse into one management pathway?

5. Responsible Management Role

Who can appropriately influence the Condition?

6. Relevant & Valuable Display

What does that role actually need to understand, decide, or act?

Intelligence Unification Principle

When multiple MI domains identify evidence related to the same
underlying management Condition, MI synthesizes that evidence into the
smallest coherent set of Intelligence Objects rather than exposing
separate domain findings as independent management problems.

39. Conflicting Domain Evidence

Domains may produce credible but conflicting explanations.

Example:

Technician/Capacity Intelligence supports a capacity constraint.

Customer/Service Experience supports a demand constraint.

If available Evidence cannot establish a primary Condition with
sufficient confidence, MI does not arbitrarily select one.

It may state:

Current evidence supports two material explanations with insufficient
confidence to establish a primary constraint.

MI then identifies the Evidence needed to resolve the uncertainty.

Diagnostic Unknown remains preferable to false certainty.

40. Priority Arbitration

When multiple genuinely independent Conditions remain after Root-Cause
Compression, MI may prioritize them using:

materiality;

urgency;

controllability;

causal reach;

expected value;

risk;

customer consequence;

safety consequence;

strategic relevance;

confidence;

management capacity.

MI does not enforce an arbitrary fixed number of priorities.

As MI improves, Root-Cause Compression should naturally reduce
management noise by moving farther upstream.

The goal is few priorities because the system has understood the
business correctly, not because software imposed a numerical limit.

ProdTracker watches what is happening in the business and first
makes sure the evidence is trustworthy. It determines what actually
